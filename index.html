From Idea to Object: printing with the Bambu P1S

This manual follows one print from the first idea to the finished object in your hand, in the order things actually happen. Each chapter ends where the next one begins. Click any chapter on the left, or any box in the diagram, to jump straight to it.

  • Build 256 × 256 × 256 mm
  • Nozzle up to 300 °C
  • Bed up to 100 °C
  • 1.75 mm filament
  • Prints .gcode.3mf

The journey at a glance

However you start, every model passes through Bambu Studio. Each box opens its chapter.

Chapter 1 of 11 · Get a model

It starts with something you want to hold

Every print begins before the printer is switched on. Someone needs a cable clip, a name tag, a replacement knob, or a decorative yin-yang for their desk. The printer cannot read your mind; it needs a 3D model, a digital description of the object's shape.

That model can come from three places, and the rest of the story depends on which one you start with:

  • A concept in your head ("a box 40 mm wide with a lid"). You will describe it to an AI or draw it in a design tool.
  • A picture (a photo of a figurine, a sketch, a logo you own). An image-to-3D AI tool can turn it into a model, or you can trace it in a design tool.
  • An existing 3D model someone else already made. You download it from a model library and go straight to slicing.

Before choosing, answer three questions about the object:

  1. How big must it be? The P1S can print anything that fits in a 256 × 256 × 256 mm cube. Larger objects must be split into parts and glued.
  2. Does it need to fit something real? A part that slides onto a pipe or clips into a slot needs measured dimensions (use calipers) and a design tool with exact numbers.
  3. Is it decorative or functional? Decorative parts forgive small shape errors; functional parts need the right material, strength and tolerances.

With those answers, pick your route: Chapter 2 (AI), Chapter 3 (download) or Chapter 4 (design it yourself).

Chapter 2 of 11 · Get a model

Route A: Asking an AI to make the model

There are two very different kinds of AI help, and they suit different objects.

A1. Chat assistants that write modelling code (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini)

A chat assistant cannot hand you a mesh directly, but it can write a script that builds one. You run the script in a free modelling program, and the program produces the STL. This is how our workshop's yin-yang was made.

  1. Describe the object precisely. Give dimensions in mm, the purpose, and the printer: "Write an OpenSCAD script for a 50 mm yin-yang coin, 3 mm thick, with a keychain hole, printable on a Bambu P1S without supports."
  2. Name the tool. Ask for OpenSCAD (simplest, exact dimensions), Blender Python (organic and artistic shapes) or CadQuery/FreeCAD Python (engineering parts).
  3. Ask for parameters at the top so you can change sizes without asking again.
  4. Run it. OpenSCAD: paste, press F5 to preview, F6 to render, then File → Export → STL. Blender: Scripting workspace → New → paste → Run Script.
  5. Iterate. If something looks wrong, describe what you see ("the hole is too close to the edge") and ask for a fix.

Best for: geometric parts, boxes, brackets, signs, name tags, anything with exact sizes.

A2. Generative 3D websites (text-to-3D and image-to-3D)

These services turn a sentence or a photo into a full 3D mesh in a few minutes. Most offer free credits after you sign in.

Service What it does Notes
Bambu MakerLab Image to 3D Photo → printable model; lets you choose Hunyuan, Meshy or Tripo engines Free to generate; downloading costs MakerWorld credits; opens straight into Bambu Studio
Meshy Text or image → 3D, exports STL/OBJ/3MF Free tier with monthly credits
Tripo AI Text or image → 3D Free credits on sign-up
Hunyuan3D Image → 3D, open-weights model Free web demo

Tips for good results:

  • Use an image with only the subject on a plain, empty background.
  • Generated models are often hollow shells, have thin fragile bits, or float above the floor. Always check them in Bambu Studio (Chapter 7) and let it repair the mesh when asked.
  • Scale the model to real millimetres before printing; AI tools often export at an arbitrary size.
  • Respect copyright: do not generate models of trademarked characters or logos you do not own.

Best for: figurines, sculptures, organic shapes, gifts. Not suited to parts that must fit precisely.

Chapter 3 of 11 · Get a model

Route B: Borrowing a model someone already made

Often the fastest route is that someone has already designed what you need. Millions of free models are shared online.

Library Why use it
MakerWorld Bambu Lab's own library. Many models come with ready-made Bambu Studio print profiles for the P1S, so you can open them and print with one click.
Printables Large, well-moderated library by Prusa; strong on functional parts.
Thingiverse The oldest and largest library; quality varies, so check the comments and "makes".
Thangs Search engine across many libraries, including geometric search for similar shapes.
Cults3D Mix of free and paid designer models.

How to choose a good model:

  1. Look at photos of real prints ("makes"), not only the designer's render.
  2. Read the comments for print problems and recommended settings.
  3. Prefer models offered as 3MF with a print profile, or at least a clean STL.
  4. Check the licence. Most are Creative Commons; "NC" (non-commercial) means you may not sell the prints, and "ND" means you may not modify the design. Keep the attribution if you share it.

Download the file and go to Chapter 5 to check its format.

Route C: Designing it yourself

When nothing online fits and AI cannot get the dimensions right, you design the part. All the tools below are free; pick by the kind of object.

Tool Runs in Licence Best for Learning curve
Tinkercad Browser Free (Autodesk account) First-timers; name tags, simple boxes, combining shapes Minutes
OpenSCAD Desktop Open source Parametric parts written as code; pairs well with AI-written scripts Hours
Blender Desktop Open source Sculpting, organic and artistic models, Python scripting Days
FreeCAD Desktop Open source Precise engineering parts with sketches and constraints Days
Onshape Browser Free plan (designs are public) Professional CAD, mechanical assemblies Days
BlocksCAD Browser Free Block-based coding, great for teaching Minutes
SketchUp Free Browser Free plan Architecture-style models, furniture Hours
Womp Browser Free plan Soft, rounded artistic shapes Hours

A few design rules make almost any model print well on an FDM printer like the P1S:

  • Give it a flat base. The first layer must stick to the plate.
  • Avoid overhangs steeper than about 45° from vertical, or plan for supports (Chapter 6).
  • Walls at least 1.2 mm thick (three nozzle widths); text raised at least 0.6 mm and 5 mm tall.
  • Leave 0.2–0.3 mm clearance between parts that must fit together.
  • Model in millimetres and export as STL or 3MF (Chapter 5).

Chapter 5 of 11 · Get a model

Getting the file into the right format

Whichever route you took, you now hold a file. There are two families of files, and mixing them up is the most common beginner confusion:

  • Model files describe the shape. You bring these to the slicer.
  • G-code describes the printer's movements: where the nozzle goes, how hot it is, how much plastic to push. The slicer creates it for you; you almost never write or download it.
Format Family What it holds Use it when
STL Model Surface triangles only; no units, no colour The universal format; every tool exports it
3MF Model + project Shape, units, colours, multiple parts, and (from Bambu Studio) full print settings Best choice for Bambu; keeps colour and settings together
OBJ Model Shape, sometimes colour/texture Common from AI and Blender; Bambu Studio imports it
STEP Model (CAD) Exact mathematical surfaces Engineering parts from FreeCAD/Onshape; Bambu Studio imports it
.gcode.3mf Print job Sliced G-code for a Bambu printer, plus preview What Bambu Studio sends to the P1S or saves to the microSD card

Rules of thumb:

  • Bring an STL, 3MF, OBJ or STEP to Bambu Studio. The printer itself needs the sliced .gcode.3mf that Bambu Studio produces.
  • Do not print G-code downloaded from the internet that was sliced for another printer. It may use the wrong temperatures or move the nozzle into the frame.
  • If a model came from an AI tool, check its size in mm on import; scale it if it arrives tiny or huge.

What the P1S accepts, and what Bambu Studio accepts

The printer and the slicer accept different files. The printer never sees your STL; it only runs the sliced job that Bambu Studio makes from it.

Where the file goes Accepted formats Notes
Bambu Studio (import) STL, 3MF, OBJ, STEP / STP, AMF, SVG SVG is a flat drawing (logo, text outline) that Studio extrudes into 3D
P1S printer (to print) .gcode.3mf (sliced plate file) Arrives wirelessly from Bambu Studio, or from the microSD card's file list
Not printable directly STL, OBJ, STEP, plain 3MF without G-code, images Must be sliced in Bambu Studio first

In short: model file → Bambu Studio → .gcode.3mf → P1S. Plain .gcode exported by other slicers is not the supported route for the P1S; slice in Bambu Studio or OrcaSlicer with the P1S profile and send the .gcode.3mf.

Chapter 6 of 11 · Prepare and slice

Thinking before you slice

The model is ready, but a good print is decided before you press Slice. Walk through these six questions in order.

1. Which way up?

The printer builds upward, one layer at a time (typically 0.2 mm), so orientation decides strength, looks and whether supports are needed.

  • Put the largest flat face on the plate.
  • Layers are weakest when pulled apart, so lay hooks and clips so the force runs along the layers, not across them.
  • The face touching the plate comes out smooth (or textured, on a textured plate); the top face shows fine lines.

2. Does it need supports?

Plastic cannot be laid on thin air. Any surface that overhangs more than about 45°, or a bridge longer than about 10 mm, needs something underneath.

  • No supports: flat-bottomed objects, reliefs, boxes, most well-designed models. Try re-orienting first.
  • Normal supports: straight columns under the overhang; strong but leave more marks.
  • Tree supports: branch-like supports that touch only where needed; easier to remove, less waste. Usually the better choice for figurines and AI-generated shapes.
  • In Bambu Studio, use support painting to add supports only where they are needed, or block them where they would scar a visible face.

3. Which material?

Filament Nozzle °C Bed °C Character Use for
PLA 190–230 55–65 Easiest, low smell, slightly brittle, softens at about 55 °C Workshop default, prototypes, decor
PETG 230–260 70–80 Tougher, slightly flexible, a little stringy Outdoor or functional parts
TPU 200–240 35–45 Rubber-like, flexible; print slowly; not through the AMS Phone cases, grips, bumpers
ABS / ASA 240–280 90–100 Heat-resistant; needs the P1S door closed and ventilation Car parts, hot environments

The P1S reaches 300 °C at the nozzle and 100 °C on the bed, so all of these work. The stock nozzle is not hardened, so avoid carbon- or glass-fibre filaments unless you fit a hardened-steel nozzle. Match the build plate to the filament: the textured PEI plate works for PLA, PETG and ABS.

4. One colour or several?

  • With an AMS (automatic material system): assign a filament to each part, or use the paint tool in Bambu Studio to colour faces. The printer swaps filament automatically.
  • Without an AMS: use a filament change at a layer height. In the Preview tab, right-click the layer slider at the height where the new colour should start, and choose Add color change (or Add pause). The printer stops, you swap the spool, and it continues. Ideal for raised text, logos and the yin-yang relief.

5. Do you want to pause mid-print?

Pausing lets you use the printer in flexible ways:

  • Embed objects: pause at the layer just above a hidden pocket, drop in a magnet, nut, coin or NFC tag, and resume. The printer seals it inside.
  • Manual colour swap, as above.
  • Check progress: you can also pause or stop any time from the printer screen, Bambu Studio's Device tab or the Bambu Handy app.

6. How strong and how fast?

  • Layer height: 0.20 mm is the standard balance; 0.12 mm for fine detail (slower); 0.28 mm for draft speed.
  • Wall loops: 2 is normal; 4 for strong parts. Walls add more strength than infill.
  • Infill: 10–15% for decor, 25–40% for functional parts. Gyroid is a good all-round pattern.
  • Brim: a thin skirt around the base that helps tall or small-footprint parts stay stuck.

Chapter 7 of 11 · Prepare and slice

Slicing: turning the shape into instructions

Slicing cuts the model into hundreds of thin horizontal layers and works out the nozzle path for each one. The result is the G-code the printer follows.

Getting to know Bambu Studio

Bambu Studio is free for Windows, macOS and Linux. It has four tabs across the top:

  • Home: recent projects and a MakerWorld browser.
  • Prepare: the build plate where you place, scale and set up models.
  • Preview: the sliced result, layer by layer, with time and filament estimates.
  • Device: live view of your printer, its camera, temperatures and controls.

Slicing step by step

  1. Choose the presets (left panel): Printer Bambu Lab P1S 0.4 nozzle; Plate type Textured PEI Plate; Filament matching the spool you will load (e.g. Bambu PLA Basic); Process 0.20 mm Standard @BBL.
  2. Import the model: drag the STL/3MF/OBJ/STEP onto the plate, or File → Import. If Studio offers to repair the mesh, accept.
  3. Check the size. Select the object and read its dimensions in the right panel. Scale if needed, keeping proportions locked.
  4. Orient it. Use Lay on face (select a flat face to put on the plate) or Auto orient. Use Arrange to space several objects.
  5. Apply your Chapter 6 decisions: supports on/off and type, wall loops, infill, brim, and per-part filament or paint colours.
  6. Slice plate. Studio switches to Preview.
  7. Inspect the preview. Drag the layer slider and look for floating islands, missing thin parts, or supports where you did not want them. Note the estimated time and grams of filament.
  8. Add pauses or colour changes on the layer slider if planned.
  9. Save the project (File → Save Project, a .3mf) so you can reprint or tweak later.

Pre-send checklist in Bambu Studio

Tick every item before you press Send. Each takes seconds, and each one catches a common failed print.

Alternatives to Bambu Studio

  • OrcaSlicer: free, open-source desktop slicer derived from Bambu Studio, with built-in P1S profiles and extra calibration tools. It can also send jobs to the P1S.
  • PrusaSlicer and Cura: open-source desktop slicers; usable with a P1S profile, but sending jobs is less convenient.
  • Kiri:Moto: free, open-source slicer that runs entirely in the browser, handy on locked-down laptops. Its output needs extra care to work on a Bambu printer, so treat it as a learning tool and use Bambu Studio or OrcaSlicer for real P1S prints.

Chapter 8 of 11 · Print

Getting the printer ready

While the file is ready on the laptop, walk over to the printer. Three things must be right: the plate, the filament, and the printer's health.

Meet the P1S

  • An enclosed box, so heat stays in and fumes stay controlled. Keep the door closed while printing.
  • Build volume 256 × 256 × 256 mm; nozzle 0.4 mm as standard, for 1.75 mm filament.
  • The hotend (nozzle heater) reaches up to 300 °C; the heatbed reaches up to 100 °C.
  • A spool holder on the back, or an optional AMS unit on top holding up to four spools.
  • A small screen with buttons on the front, a microSD card slot, Wi-Fi and a camera.

Prepare the build plate

  1. Lift the flexible steel plate off the magnetic bed.
  2. Wash it with warm water and dish soap, rinse, and dry fully. Fingerprints are the number-one cause of prints not sticking.
  3. Put it back, pressing its rear notches against the two pins at the back of the bed so it sits square.
  4. Make sure the plate in the printer matches the plate type chosen in Bambu Studio.

Load the filament (external spool, no AMS)

  1. Hang the spool on the rear holder so it unwinds without twisting.
  2. Cut the filament end at an angle with flush cutters for a sharp tip, and straighten the first 10 cm.
  3. Push the end into the PTFE tube at the back until it reaches the extruder at the top of the toolhead.
  4. On the screen, open Filament → Load. The printer heats the nozzle to the filament's temperature (around 220 °C for PLA), which takes about a minute.
  5. When prompted, keep pushing gently until you feel the extruder gears grab the filament and pull it in.
  6. The printer purges a short length through the nozzle and drops it down the waste chute at the back. When clean, new-colour plastic comes out, loading is done.
  7. To change spools later, use Filament → Unload: it heats the nozzle and pushes the filament back out.

With an AMS, simply feed the filament end into a free slot; the AMS pulls it in, reads Bambu RFID spools automatically, and loads the right slot when a print starts.

Quick health check

  • The nozzle is clean, with no blob of old plastic stuck to it.
  • Nothing is lying inside the printer or on the plate.
  • The waste chute at the back is not clogged.

Chapter 9 of 11 · Print

Sending the job to the printer

The sliced plate is on the laptop; now it has to reach the P1S. There are exactly two ways: wirelessly over Wi-Fi, or by hand on a microSD card.

Way Needs Good for
1. Wireless Laptop and P1S on the same Wi-Fi network, Bambu Studio, the printer's IP address and access code Everyday printing; live camera and controls in Bambu Studio
2. microSD card A microSD card and a card reader No network available, or Wi-Fi is down

Way 1 — Wirelessly, over the same Wi-Fi

Bambu Studio talks to the printer directly across the Wi-Fi network. It identifies the printer by its IP address and proves it is allowed to use it with the access code.

  1. Connect the printer to Wi-Fi. On the P1S screen, open Settings → WLAN (network settings) and join the network.
  2. Note the IP address and access code. Both are shown in the printer's network settings. The access code is the printer's password; anyone with it on the same network can control the printer.
  3. Connect the laptop to the same Wi-Fi network. Many guest and office networks block devices from seeing each other ("client isolation"). If the printer never appears, ask IT for a network that allows it, or use a dedicated router for the printer.
  4. Insert a microSD card in the printer. When printing over the local network, the job is stored on the card before it prints.
  5. Add the printer in Bambu Studio. Open the Device tab. The printer appears in the list; select it and enter the access code. If it does not appear, add it manually with its IP address and the access code.
  6. Send the job. After slicing, click Print plate. In the dialog, check the printer, keep Bed leveling ticked, choose timelapse on or off, and (with an AMS) check the filament-to-slot mapping. Click Send.
  7. Watch it. The Device tab now shows the printer's camera, temperatures and progress, with pause and stop buttons.

Optional: logging into a Bambu Lab account in Bambu Studio and the Bambu Handy app also lets you watch the print from your phone when you are away from the office network. Sending still works the same way.

Way 2 — By hand, on a microSD card

  1. After slicing, choose Export plate sliced file (the dropdown next to Print plate). This saves a .gcode.3mf file.
  2. Copy the file to a microSD card (FAT32 formatted works best) using a card reader.
  3. Insert the card into the printer's slot.
  4. On the printer screen, open the SD card file list, select your file, and press Print.

A plain STL on the card will not print. The printer only understands the sliced .gcode.3mf from Bambu Studio.

Chapter 10 of 11 · Print

The print comes to life

The job has arrived. Nothing comes out of the nozzle for the first several minutes; that is normal. The printer is getting itself ready.

The preparation sequence

Times below are typical for PLA and will vary with filament, room temperature and settings.

Stage What happens Typical time
1. Bed heating The heatbed warms to the filament's bed temperature (about 55–65 °C for PLA). Hotter filaments like ABS at 90–100 °C take noticeably longer. 1–5 min
2. Homing and nozzle wipe The toolhead finds its zero position and wipes the nozzle on the brush at the back. Under 1 min
3. Auto bed leveling The nozzle probes many points on the plate to map tiny height differences, so the first layer is even. 2–4 min
4. Nozzle heating The hotend heats to printing temperature (about 220 °C for PLA). It often overlaps with the earlier steps. About 1 min
5. Purge line A short line of plastic is drawn at the front edge to prime the nozzle. Seconds
6. First layer The slowest, most important layer. A few minutes

In total, plan on roughly 5–10 minutes from Send to the first layer. Bambu Studio's time estimate covers the printing itself, so add this preparation time when planning a session.

Why heat matters

  • Nozzle heating melts the filament so it can flow. Too cold gives weak, under-extruded layers; too hot gives stringing and blobs.
  • Bed heating keeps the bottom layers warm and slightly soft so they grip the plate and do not shrink and peel up (warping).
  • The enclosure traps warm air. That helps ABS and ASA, but for PLA in a warm room, opening the top lid slightly can prevent heat creep.

Watching the print

  1. Stay for the first layer. It should be flat, slightly squashed and continuous. Gaps, loose strands or curling corners mean you should stop, clean the plate and try again.
  2. Watch remotely through the camera in Bambu Studio's Device tab or the Bambu Handy app.
  3. Pause or stop from the printer screen, the Device tab or the app at any time.
  4. At a planned colour-change or pause layer, the printer parks the toolhead and beeps. Swap the filament (Unload → Load) or place your insert, then press Resume.
  5. Do not open the door or reach in while it moves. The nozzle is over 200 °C and the bed can be 100 °C.

Chapter 11 of 11 · Print

The object in your hand

The printer beeps, the toolhead parks, and the story reaches its end, almost.

Taking the print off

  1. Wait a few minutes for the bed to cool. PLA parts often release by themselves once cool.
  2. Lift the flexible plate out and gently flex it; the part pops off. Avoid prying with metal tools on the plate surface.
  3. Remove supports and brims with flush cutters and pliers, cutting away from your hands.
  4. Clean up edges with a deburring tool or fine sandpaper if needed.
  5. Put the plate back, clear any purge waste, and leave the printer ready for the next person.

When things go wrong

What you see Likely cause What to do
Print comes loose or corners lift Greasy plate, small base, cold room Wash the plate with soap; add a brim; close the door
Spaghetti mess mid-print Part detached or no support under an overhang Stop the print; re-orient or add supports
Thin strings between parts Wet filament or too hot Dry the filament; use the correct filament preset
Missing layers or weak, gappy walls Clog or tangled spool Check the spool unwinds freely; unload and reload filament
Printer not visible in Bambu Studio Different network or LAN access blocked Check both are on the same Wi-Fi; re-enter the access code; add the printer by IP; or use the microSD card
File on microSD card not listed STL copied instead of sliced file Export plate sliced file (.gcode.3mf) from Bambu Studio
Model is the wrong size AI or download used other units Scale in Bambu Studio before slicing

Safety and care

  • Hot parts: the nozzle exceeds 200 °C and the bed reaches 100 °C.
  • Keep PLA as the default in shared offices; print ABS/ASA only with good ventilation.
  • Store filament sealed with desiccant; damp filament prints badly.
  • Clean the plate regularly; keep the printer's firmware and Bambu Studio up to date.

That is the full journey: an idea became a model, the model became layers, and the layers became an object you can hold. The next print is easier.
